How the fly decides where to go

Eyes (model). When the goal is in view on the fly’s left, the left LC10a neurons fire (115 cells, the visual projection neurons flies use to track objects); the further off-centre it is, the faster they fire. Walls close to an eye loom, which drives that side’s LPLC2 looming detectors.

Brain (connectome, not trained). Those firing rates are fed into a leaky integrate-and-fire simulation of all 138,639 neurons and 15 million connections, using the parameters of Shiu et al. (Nature 2024). No weights are fitted. In this wiring diagram LC10a excites the same-side steering neuron DNa02, so the fly turns toward the goal. LPLC2 excites the opposite-side DNa01 and DNa02, so it turns away from a looming wall.

Legs (model). The fly walks forward, turns at a rate set by left-minus-right activity of DNa01 + DNa02, and wanders a little at random. Each steering neuron is read relative to its own peak rate, measured once at start-up, because the reconstructed left DNa02 is about 4× more excitable than the right one. When the goal is hidden behind walls the fly has nothing to steer by except wall avoidance, so it wanders until it catches sight of the goal again.
